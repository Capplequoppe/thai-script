## exposition three-market-letters
heading: A plate, a turtle and a fish
- จ, jaaw jaan, is a hook curving up and over from a head at mid height — a plate spun on one finger at a noodle stall. The sound is j with no puff behind it, halfway toward the ch of ช.
- ต, dtaaw dtào, is ด with a notch cut into the rim — the dent in the shell where the turtle pulled its head in. The sound is dt: press d and t into one flat sound and let no breath out.
- ป, bpaaw bplaa, is บ with the right wall grown up past the rim — a fish clearing the market basket. The sound is bp, b and p pressed flat together.
- All three stand in the market. Mid class, every one of them, and that is the whole point of teaching them together.

## exposition why-the-market
heading: Why the market is the easy district
- Listen to what จ ต ป have in common with ก ด บ from lesson three: every one of them stops, and none of them releases breath. Hold a hand up and say ปี (bpii), a year. Nothing lands on it.
- That is the market's rule, and it has no exceptions. A Thai consonant that stops and releases no breath is mid class. You do not memorise the list; you listen for the missing puff.
- Compare พ from lesson five, which stops and does breathe, and ม which does not stop at all. Three behaviours, three districts.

## retrieval market-or-not
reveal: market-or-not-answer
prompt: Someone hands you a Thai letter you have never seen and tells you it is pronounced dt. Which district is it in, and how sure can you be?

## reveal market-or-not-answer
retrieval: market-or-not
- The market. Mid class.
- Completely sure, without seeing the letter. A plain unaspirated stop is mid class with no exceptions, so the sound alone settles it.

## exposition o-vowels
heading: โ and โ-ะ, the leaning mast
- โ is a mast whose tip loops once and leans forward over the letter. It is written in front like เ and แ, and it is a long round oo with the mouth in a full circle.
- โต (dtoo) is to grow or to be large. โลก (lôok) is the world. โดย (dooi) is by or by means of.
- โ-ะ is the short one, written with the loop in front and the stacked hooks after — and it is the vowel you have already been reading without knowing it.

## exposition the-vowel-you-cannot-see
heading: The short o that is written with nothing
- Put a final consonant after โ-ะ and the spelling does not shrink to a roof the way เ-ะ and แ-ะ do. It disappears completely.
- คน (khon) from lesson six is exactly this: ค, nothing, น, and the nothing is a short o. So is ตก (dtòk), to fall, and จน (jon), until.
- That is not a quirk of three words. Two bare consonants side by side with no vowel written between them are read with this vowel, every time, and the full rule — including what happens with three consonants — has a lesson to itself.
- Say it as one syllable. คน is khon in a single beat, never kho-no.

## retrieval nothing-between
reveal: nothing-between-answer
prompt: You meet a two-letter word with no vowel written anywhere in it. What do you put between the letters, and how many syllables is it?

## reveal nothing-between-answer
retrieval: nothing-between
- The short o of โ-ะ, unwritten.
- One syllable. The first letter is the initial, the second is the final, and the vowel between them is never spelled out.

## exposition more-words
heading: Words you can read now
- จะ (jà) marks the future and is the third commonest word in Thai. จาก (jàak) is from. ตาม (dtaam) is to follow or according to.
- เป็น (bpen) is to be — the mast, the roof and a market letter, all three of this course's last two lessons in one very common word.
- ตา (dtaa) is an eye, ตี (dtii) is to hit, ปาก (bpàak) is a mouth, and จับ (jàp) is to catch.
- Some of these end dead — จาก, ปาก, จับ, ตก — and you cannot yet say what tone they take. Mid class on a dead syllable is the one rule of the four still missing, and it arrives in lesson eleven.
