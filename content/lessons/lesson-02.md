WHO IS SPEAKING. Ajarn Pim — see docs/the-teacher.md. Born above a working
harbour in Songkhla, left at seven, sang the alphabet for twenty years without
reading a word of it, learned to read at twenty-six. This lesson is hers to
say, so the count openers ("Three consonants and one tone rule…") and the bare
ordinals ("The first letter." / "The second letter." / "The last of the
three.") are gone. She is allowed to be pleased to be back at the snake, and
she is allowed one sentence about her own harbour where the ring story earns
it. One sentence. It is not a set piece.

THE SHAPE OF A LESSON. The skeleton is meet → story → see → write, and it is
varied where the material asks rather than for novelty:

  1. What is in this lesson — as a thing to open, never as an inventory.
  2. Whatever makes the set cheaper than it looks. Here: one district covers
     all three, so the class is learned once instead of three times.
  3. Each symbol, one at a time. Name and meaning, then the story across
     several pictures, then the glyph and the hand.
  4. The rules, each with its own story.
  5. Retrieval, then reading back.

THE PUZZLE BITES FIRST. `see-waen` asks the learner to hum all five letters
they now own and listen for what the five have in common; `live-endings`
releases it on the next slide. The slide turn is the pause. An earlier draft
asked and answered inside one paragraph, which left nothing to feel.

PEN AND PAPER. Lesson 1 stops the lesson and sends the learner for a pen, and
until this rewrite lesson 2 never asked them to write anything at all — three
letters met and none of them drawn, which is the gap people fall through. The
writing now rides on the `see-` slide of each letter, the way lesson 1's
`see-nu` carries it, so no slide was added and no picture changed. Both halves
of the name are said aloud while the hand is moving.

THE MARKET VENDOR IN THE PADDY. The old text promised he was "there for a rule
you have not met yet", that the learner would "find him already waiting", and
that the picture "you will meet again every time this rule comes up". None of
the three was true: lessons 3 and 4 reuse the paddy with the people removed
and nothing ever collects him. The promise is now small enough to keep — he is
in the field for the same reason the fisherman is, nothing has happened to him
either, and where he is from is next lesson. Lesson 3 teaches the market, mid
class, and mid class on a live ending lands flat, so the promise pays in one
lesson rather than never.

WHY STORIES RATHER THAN DESCRIPTION. A shape cue tells a learner what to look
at; it gives them no reason to care. These stories follow one recipe:

  * something happens to a person, and it matters;
  * the letter's *sound* comes out of that event — usually a cry, or a noise
    the harbour itself makes;
  * the letter's *word* is the thing at the centre of it;
  * it ends with an object left behind in the district, so the letter has an
    address as well as a meaning.

Glyph geometry is woven in where it survives being told — a snake's head
rising out of a coil really is ง, and the giant's chin and belly really are
ย's two bumps — and dropped where keeping it would cost the story. A learner
who can see the snake will find the curve again; one who can see a curve and
no snake has nothing to hold.

แหวน IS JEWELLERY. A band worn on a finger. `consonant-scenes.json` calls it a
life-ring in ว's palace narration, which is wrong and is not this file's to
fix; the lesson says silver band and finger every time it says it at all.

TWO RULES STILL GOVERN NARRATION, unchanged from lesson 1.

1. An `en` line contains no Thai — no glyphs, no romanisation. An English
   mouth making a Thai sound badly teaches the wrong target, and in a tonal
   language that is worse than useless. English describes and points; every
   Thai sound comes from a `th` clip. So English never says "this letter is
   called X"; it says "listen", and the Thai voice says it.

2. A bare open syllable is voiced twice as one token — นานา, not นา. The Thai
   model renders a lone open syllable with the wrong tone five times in six.
   Measured; see lesson 1's header. Watch the doubling for real words: มามา
   comes back as มาม่า, the noodle brand.

- She worked the rope locker out where the pier runs to sea.
- The snake lived in the coils and kept the rats off the nets.
- It is why last lesson's mouse lived at the *other* end of the quay.

## rule live-endings
rule: live-endings
narration: en Did you hear it? Every one of those five is a sound you can hum. Start any of them and you can hold it until your breath gives out, which is precisely why a word is allowed to finish on one — the sound has somewhere to keep going. That is what alive means. You have been hearing the word since lesson one without being told what decides it. Five letters in the whole alphabet can end a syllable alive, and you now own all five of them.
narration: en A syllable that ends on a long vowel with nothing behind it stays open the same way. Here is one of those.
narration: th นา
narration: en Nothing closes it. The air simply keeps going. An ending that stops the air dead is the other kind. You meet that one next lesson.
