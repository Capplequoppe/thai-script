## exposition borrowed-letters
heading: Six letters that mark a borrowed word
- Thai keeps extra letters for words it took from Sanskrit and Pali. They double sounds you already read, and seeing one is a hint that the word is old, formal, or borrowed.
- ศ and ษ are temple s, like ส. ณ is harbor n, like น. ญ is harbor y, like ย. ภ is harbor ph, like พ. ธ is harbor th, like ท.
- ศึกษา (sùek-sǎa) — to study — carries two of them at once. ภาพ (phâap) is a picture. เธอ (thooe) is you. คุณ (khun) — you, polite — ends on ณ.

## exposition telling-them-apart
heading: Reading the six on sight
- ศ leans forward with a split head; ษ carries a broken crown. Both live uphill in the temple.
- ณ is น with a long tail folded under it. ญ is ย standing over a small base. ภ is a steep sail; ธ is a flag on a pole.
- ญาติ (yâat) is a relative. เงิน (ngoen) is money — harbor ง, level tone, and a vowel written in a way this lesson explains below.

## exposition temple-dead
heading: The temple's other half: dead syllables fall low
- A temple letter on a live syllable rises — that was the last lesson. On a dead syllable it gives a low tone, short vowel or long.
- หก (hòk) — six — is temple ห stopped by k: low. In ศึกษา (sùek-sǎa) the first half is temple ศ stopped by k, so it comes out low; the second half ends open and live, so it rises. One word, both temple rules.

## exposition sara-am
heading: ำ — the vowel with a built-in ending
- The sign ำ is a circle-then-hook written after its consonant, and it packs a vowel and a final m into one shape.
- ทำ (tham) is to do. จำ (jam) is to remember. Both end in m with no ม written anywhere — the vowel brought its own.

## exposition oe-vowel
heading: เ-อ — the oe vowel, and the shape it takes before a final
- เ-อ wraps its consonant: เ in front, อ after. เธอ (thooe) is you; เจอ (jooe) is to meet.
- Add a final consonant and the อ steps aside for ิ above: เงิน (ngoen), money, and เดิน (dooen), to walk, are เ-อ words in that closed shape.
- The short mate เ-อะ clips it, and is rare in running text.

## exposition gaaran
heading: The gaa-ran sign — the silencer
- Borrowed words often keep letters Thai does not say. The sign ์ above a letter switches it off.
- สัตว์ (sàt) — animal — writes a ว and switches it off: a temple letter, a stopped ending, a low tone, and one letter kept purely for the eye.

## retrieval two-halves
reveal: two-halves-answer
prompt: ศึกษา (to study) is read sùek-sǎa — low then rising. Both halves open with a temple s. What makes the two tones come out different?

## reveal two-halves-answer
retrieval: two-halves
- Live against dead. The first syllable is stopped by k — dead, so the temple gives it low. The second ends open on a long vowel — live, so the temple gives it rising.

## retrieval silent-letter
reveal: silent-letter-answer
prompt: สัตว์ (animal) is read sàt, one closed syllable. Its spelling ends with ว under a small sign. What is that sign doing, and what would the word lose without it?

## reveal silent-letter-answer
retrieval: silent-letter
- That is gaa-ran, the silencer, switching off the ว. Without it the spelling would ask you to read the ว; with it the borrowed spelling survives on paper while the mouth says sàt.

## exposition close
heading: What you can now read
- The six loanword letters ศ ษ ณ ญ ภ ธ, the packed vowel ำ, เ-อ in both written shapes, and the silencer ์.
- And the temple's tone behaviour is complete: rising when live, low when dead.
