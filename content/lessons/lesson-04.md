## exposition breath-pair
heading: A letter that breathes and a letter that hisses
- chaaw cháang pushes ch out on a burst of breath. Palm up again: this one is supposed to move it.
- saaw sôo is a flat hiss with no burst behind it at all — air escaping, not air thrown.
- Both are new kinds of sound for you. Every letter you have met so far either hummed or stopped; these two do neither.

## exposition near-twins
heading: Two shapes you have to keep apart
- ช climbs in one smooth unbroken stroke, with a tail flicking up past the top line.
- ซ is the same figure with a notch dented into that climbing stroke — one link of a chain snagged in it — and the same tail above.
- The notch is the entire difference. Find it before you read the word, not after.

## retrieval spot-the-notch
reveal: spot-the-notch-answer
prompt: ช and ซ differ by one detail. Name it, and say which of the two carries it.

## reveal spot-the-notch-answer
retrieval: spot-the-notch
- The notch dented into the climbing stroke.
- ซ has it. ช runs smooth.

## exposition short-vowels
heading: Two vowels that are over before they start
- ะ is a pair of small hooks written after the consonant, and it clips the vowel short. นะ (ná) is the particle that softens whatever you just said.
- ิ is the beret you learned last lesson with its upright flick removed — a quick i, gone before it settles. ซิ (sí) urges someone on: go on, do it.
- Short is not a shade of long here. It is a different vowel, and next rule it decides a tone.

rule: mai-han-akat

## exposition han-akat
heading: ะ in disguise
- ะ sits after its consonant, so a final consonant arriving behind it has nowhere to stand. Thai solves it by lifting the vowel out of the way: it becomes a single curl, ั, written above the initial.
- วัน (wan) is a day. กัน (gan) is together. ดัง (dang) is loud.
- Nothing about the sound changed. Only where it is written changed, which is why it is worth learning as one vowel with two spellings rather than as two vowels.

## rule low-dead-short
rule: low-dead-short

## exposition high-tone
heading: Low class, stopped short
- A low class letter, a syllable stopped dead, and a short vowel: the pitch jumps to the top of your range.
- นะ (ná) is exactly that — ะ is short and nothing follows it, which counts as stopped. So is ซิ (sí).
- วัด (wát) is to measure: the same short ั, now sealed dead by ด.
- นัก (nák) marks a person who does something as a calling, and it stops on the k-stop of ก.

## exposition live-contrast
heading: The same letters, ending live
- ชาย (chaai) is a man and ชาว (chaao) is the people of a place. Both open on ช, which is low class, and both end on a hum.
- Live ending, so the low class live rule applies and they land flat — not high.
- One letter, two tones, decided entirely by how the syllable ends. That is the machine you are learning.

## retrieval two-endings
reveal: two-endings-answer
prompt: ชาย and ซิ both open on a low class letter. One lands flat and one lands high. Say which is which and name the two things that decide it.

## reveal two-endings-answer
retrieval: two-endings
- ชาย lands flat: it ends on ย, a hum, so the syllable is live.
- ซิ lands high: the vowel is short and nothing follows it, so the syllable is dead.
- The ending and the vowel length decide it. The class was the same in both.
