# One breath, one hiss, and the short a in disguise

lesson: lesson-04

<!--
Opening band, lesson 4 of 5. Declares ช and ซ, and the short vowels ะ, ั and ิ.
previews: none

THE TWO LETTERS ARE ONE STORY. ซ is ช with a notch dented into the climbing
stroke, and consonant-scenes.json already says what the notch is: one link of
chain snagged in it. So the elephant hauls boats on a chain, and the two
letters are the two halves of one job. Learn the elephant and the chain comes
almost free, which is the same bargain the market's three stops offered.

The sounds come out of the same picture. The elephant blows — a burst of
breath through the trunk, which is what ช is. The chain hisses flat across
stone, which is what ซ is. Breath against no breath, and the learner has
already done the palm test on the market's three.

THE SHORTENER IS THE LESSON. ะ makes a vowel short every time it appears, and
this is the one lesson where both halves of that rule can be shown rather than
asserted: ะ arrives short, and ิ arrives short without it. So the rule is
stated in the only direction it runs, and its own counterexample is on the
next slide.

THE SHORTENER'S NAME IS THE RULE. The back yard's lodger is อัด, which means to
compress, and it is written carrier + mai han akat + final consonant — the
climb this lesson teaches, in his own name. A learner who understands why he
is spelled that way has understood the rule.

MNEMONICS ARE TAKEN, NOT FORCED, unchanged from lesson 3. The chain is
snagged, slack and sliding, and every one of those is the word you would have
reached for. The elephant gets none: English has no ch-word that describes it
without reaching, and a forced chain costs more than it pays.
-->

## exposition the-cheat-code
image: images/lesson-04/the-cheat-code.jpg
scene: A man in a straw hat leaning back against a closed wooden gate at the edge of a swept yard, arms folded, evening light across dry grass, distant hills beyond.
heading: The best shortcut you will get
teaches: sara-a
narration: en Here is why he is worth more than the two letters. There are thirty vowels in Thai and every one of them is either long or short, and getting that wrong changes the word. Thirty things to learn — except that nine of them simply carry him on the end, and every single time he is there, the vowel is short. So a third of the problem is solved by looking. But listen to how that rule runs, because it runs one way only. Seeing him tells you it is short. His absence tells you nothing at all.
- Every vowel is long or short, and it changes the word.
- **If he is on the end, it is short.** Nine of the thirty carry him.
- **If he is absent, the vowel may still be either.**

## exposition han-akat
image: images/lesson-04/han-akat.jpg
scene: A man in a straw hat halfway up a ladder to the roof of a Thai house on stilts, looking back down at a loaded handcart parked squarely across the swept yard below leaving no room to stand, evening light.
heading: When the back yard is taken
teaches: mai-han-akat, sara-a
narration: en One thing about the back yard. It is small, and it is where a final consonant stands too. So when a syllable has a short vowel and something after it, both of them want the same spot. Which of the two do you think moves? The vowel does. He goes up on the roof instead, and he leaves a different mark there — a single curl above the first letter. The sound is the same and the length is the same, and the curl is what you read them from.
narration: th วัน
narration: en A day. And that is his own name doing it, by the way. When you heard his name a minute ago there was a consonant on the end of it, so the back yard was taken, so his own name is written with the curl on the roof.
- A final consonant stands in the back yard too, and gets there first.
- So the vowel climbs: one curl, written above the first letter.
- Same sound, same length, written in a different place.
