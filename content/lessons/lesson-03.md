# Three stops with no breath, long ii, and where a syllable dies

lesson: lesson-03

<!--
Opening band, lesson 3 of 5. Declares ก, ด and บ, and the long vowel ี.
previews: none

THE MARKET IS THE QUIET DISTRICT. All three of this lesson's letters release
no breath, and all three stop a syllable dead. Rather than teach those as two
separate facts about three separate letters, the market has a reason to be
quiet — a child asleep under a stall — and the three letters are the three
sounds that do not wake him. The harbour let sounds run on; the market stops
them. That contrast is the lesson.

THE CHILD IS A PROMISE THE LESSON KEEPS. He used to be introduced with a bare
"Remember him", and he never came back as himself. The instruction is gone.
What stands in its place is something to notice on `gai-story-1` — a market
fills up around this boy and he sleeps through it — which `dek-story-2`
answers, and which the closing slide collects: the last word the learner reads
is spelled on his letter. A learner told to remember somebody is owed a reason
inside the lesson that told them.

THE VOWEL HOUSE ARRIVES HERE, because ี is the first vowel taught after the
one lesson 1 gave away. See docs/vowel-house.md. It gets one slide of its own
before the lodger, because this is the only lesson that has to introduce the
whole scheme; every later lesson just opens a room.

Pim owns the naming rule out loud on `meet-ik` — everyone in the house is
named after the sound they make, which is her invention and not a fact about
Thai. She does not also claim the house on `the-vowel-house`: lesson 2 already
spends that move on the districts, and running it twice in two lessons turns a
confession into a tic. See docs/the-teacher.md, which is the brief for who is
speaking.

MNEMONICS ARE TAKEN, NOT FORCED. Where the letter's own sound gives you the
words, say the chain out loud and let it be heard — glorious golden grain for
the chicken, drowsy, dreaming, deep in the dark for the child, one big, bright,
beautiful leaf out of a bucket. Lesson 1's "nocturnal, notorious,
knot-nibbling neighbour" is the model. It works because a rodent hands English
more N-words than anyone needs, and every word in it is still the word you
would have reached for anyway.

That last part is the test. A chain earns its place when the alliteration is
free — when the words describe the thing accurately and happen to start with
the right letter. Where they do not, there is no chain, and that is not a gap
to fill: a forced one costs a learner more to hold than the letter it was
meant to help with. ง gets none, and the rope-maker's daughter carries it on
story alone.

It follows that naming a story's people after its letter is out. English names
teach an English sound, which is right for ม and น and wrong for ก, ด and บ —
a chicken called Gary would unteach the slide it stands on. A chain describes;
a name claims a pronunciation.

NAMES. The roof's lodger is อีก, and the name is the sound — a Thai name
beginning with a vowel is written with อ as a silent carrier, so the first
syllable is the vowel exactly. It is said by the Thai voice, never the
narrator, for the same reason no other Thai is: an English mouth on a Thai
name teaches the wrong target.

THE ALPHABET SONG IS ORIENTATION'S, not this lesson's. Pim tells the whole of
it on the `welcome` slide there — grandmother, seven, forty-four names sung in
order for twenty years, a shop sign she could not read — and that slide's
argument about re-reading rests on it, so it is not moving.

What orientation cannot say is which letter the song starts on: it names no
letter and prints no glyph anywhere. That is the half `meet-gai` owns, and it
is the whole of what this lesson spends on the subject — the song is referred
back to ("that song I told you about") rather than retold. Three phrases are
welcome's and must stay out of here: "forty-four names at seven", "twenty-six",
and the point that a song and an alphabet are different things.

Her own life is rationed across the course rather than pooled. Orientation
holds Songkhla, the harbour's smell, the nephew, the doctorate and twenty-six;
lesson 2 holds why the low-class letters got a harbour. This lesson spends
two small ones nobody else has: a month lost to reading without writing
(`pen-still-there`), and a year of unaspirated stops nobody was willing to
correct (`no-puff-answer`).

## exposition what-is-here
image: images/lesson-03/what-is-here.jpg
scene: A Thai open-air market at first light, wooden stalls under striped awnings, baskets of produce stacked and covered, no shoppers yet, long shadows.
heading: Inland, to where the noise stops
narration: en Up out of the harbour today and a little way inland, to a place where everybody is being careful not to make much noise. Why they are being careful is the first thing I would like you to work out for yourself. What you collect while you are there is three consonants and a vowel. The consonants are so nearly the same animal that once you have one of them properly the other two come along behind it. They live in the same place, they are made with the same part of your mouth, and all three do the same thing to the end of a word. Every letter you have met so far lets a sound carry on ringing after it, so you could hold the end of a word for as long as you had breath in you. These three stop the sound dead. And once a syllable can finish in two different ways the tone rules stop agreeing with each other, so you get two of those today rather than one.
- Three consonants, one long vowel, two rules.
- The three are alike enough that one of them teaches the others.
- Last lesson's letters let sounds ring on. These stop them.

## exposition the-trick

heading: The quiet end of the market
narration: en So. The market. Two lessons down at the water and this is the other place you will keep coming back to, so stand in it a while before you meet anybody.
