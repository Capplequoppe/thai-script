THE SHAPE OF A LESSON.

  1. What is in this lesson, and why it is worth the trouble.
  2. The tricks, before any of the work.
  3. Each symbol: sound, name, story, glyph, and then writing it by hand.
  4. The rules, each with its own story.
  5. Retrieval, then reading back.

WHY STORIES RATHER THAN DESCRIPTION. A shape cue tells a learner what to look
at; it gives them no reason to care. These stories follow one recipe:

  * something happens to a person, and it matters;
  * the letter's *sound* comes out of that event — usually a cry, or a noise
    the harbour itself makes;
  * the letter's *word* is the thing at the centre of it;
  * it ends with an object left behind in the district, so the letter has an
    address as well as a meaning.

The method is explained to the learner directly, once, before the first story:
put yourself in the scene, use every sense, let it land emotionally. A learner
who does not know they are supposed to do that will read a story instead of
building a memory.

WRITING BY HAND IS NOT OPTIONAL. The lesson stops, tells the learner to fetch
paper and a pen, and waits. Seeing a stroke order explained is not the same as
executing it, and skipping that is the most common way people fail at this.

ROMANISATION — THE ONE DELIBERATE EXCEPTION.
The standing rule is that an `en` narration line contains no Thai and no
romanisation: an English mouth making a Thai sound badly teaches the wrong
target, and in a tonal language the learner then practises against it.

Two things in this lesson cross that line, both on purpose and both recorded
here so a later edit does not "fix" them by accident:

  * The naming pattern is explained with *English* letters — S, P and J said
    as "Saw, Paw, Jaw", and the shared vowel described as the word "awe".
    These are English sounds used to describe an English speaker's own mouth,
    not attempts at Thai.
  * The boy in the horse story shouts "Ma!". In the story that is a child's
    word for his mother, in English, and it happens to land on the sound the
    lesson is teaching. That coincidence is the engine of the whole mnemonic.

Everything else obeys the rule. English never says "this letter is called X";
it says "listen", and a `th` clip says it.

BARE OPEN SYLLABLES ARE NO LONGER DOUBLED, and the reason they were is worth
keeping. `นา` used to be written `นานา`, because the metered engine rendered a
bare open syllable as น่า — a different word — on six seeds out of six, at both
stability settings and on both Thai voices the account could reach. Repeating
the syllable fixed it five times in six, and a closed syllable such as `นาน`
never failed at all.

That was one workaround. There are now two, and they do not belong together:
short Thai is spoken inside a carrier sentence and cut back out on word
timings, which solves the same problem without the learner hearing anything
twice. Running both taught that a rice field is called "na na".

The narration outlived the doubling by one pass. `word-naa` still said "you
heard it twice there, which is how a teacher would drill it" while the clip
said the word once — the manifest has a single verified `นา` — so the sentence
is gone. If the carrier-sentence trick is ever backed out, the narration has to
move with it.

LOOP-DIRECTION COUNTS, AND WHY THE NARRATION DOES NOT GIVE THEM.
The figures behind the claim are 45 clockwise (29 consonants, 9 vowels, 7
digits) against 18 counter-clockwise (11 consonants, 4 vowels, 3 digits). They
are recorded here and deliberately kept out of the lesson: a learner needs to
know that clockwise is the safe default, and nothing follows for them from
nine of the clockwise symbols being vowels.

Keeping them out also keeps an unverified number off the screen. The
arithmetic is self-consistent and the digits account for all ten, but the
consonant figures total 40, which would mean four of the forty-four carry no
head at all. Nothing in this repository records loop direction as data, so
that is unconfirmed — and "less than half as many" stays true whichever way
the four fall.

Scene grammar is inherited from symbols.ts, not reinvented: the harbour is the
low class.

BOTH LETTERS HAVE TWO LOOPS, AND THEY ARE NOT MIRRORS. symbols.ts used to say
that each had one loop and that น was ม mirrored, and this lesson repeated it.
Render the glyphs and the claim collapses: ม stacks both loops on the left,
น puts one high-left and one low-right. Mirroring ม would move both loops to
the right, which is no letter at all. The shared upper-left loop is the head
that nearly every Thai consonant starts with, so it can never be the thing
that tells two of them apart. The lower loop is the only discriminator.

ILLUSTRATIONS CARRY MEANING, NEVER LETTERFORM. A scene must not be asked to
draw the shape of a glyph, and it must not be asked to render text: the plaque
on the wooden horse is in the scene because the object belongs there, and the
narration says what is written on it, because the render will not.

present. It scores subjects, not geometry, so it cannot catch this and will
wave it through. The glyph itself, set large on its own slide, draws its shape
perfectly. The picture's job is the sound and the meaning.

# The harbour's two hums

lesson: lesson-01

## exposition overview
heading: What is in this lesson, and why it matters
narration: en Right. Last time you got the systems — how a Thai symbol is written, how a Thai letter is named — and no letters at all to use them on. That was deliberate, and it stops now. Today two consonants go in, and one vowel, and by the end of it you will be reading three real Thai words. Not recognising them. Reading them, out loud, on the right tone, having built each one yourself out of parts you will have met a few minutes earlier.
narration: en There is one more thing in here that nobody warns you about, and it is a habit in your own voice rather than anything on the page. We will get to it when you can hear it. And everything you learn today lands somewhere — a real place, with weather and noise and people in it. That is the part that sounds strange until it happens to you.
- Two consonants, one vowel.
- Three real Thai words, built rather than memorised.
- One habit of the voice.
- And a place to keep it all.

## exposition meet-ma
glyph: ม
anchor: MMM
gloss: horse letter
cue: the long call across the water
heading: Your first consonant, and what alive means
teaches: mo-ma
narration: en Close your lips and hum through your nose. Keep going — hold it as long as you have breath for. That is your first Thai consonant. You have owned the sound since before you could talk, because Thai does nothing surprising to an M. Now try the same thing with a P. Go on, try to hold a P. [pause] You cannot. It happens and it is finished. The air stops dead behind it. That difference has a name. It is also the first piece of grammar in this course. A sound you can hold is alive. A sound that stops is dead. Only a handful of consonants are alive. This one is among them, and I am pointing it out now because it comes back in a very big way when the tone rules arrive.
- The Thai version of **M**.
- Close your lips and hum through your nose.
- You already own this sound.
- **Alive** — a sound you can hold as long as you have breath.

recording: th มอ ม้า public/audio/consonant-mo-ma.mp3
narration: en The word on the end of it means horse. Hold on to that, because the next few minutes are about one particular horse. Now the part I actually love doing. I am going to tell you a story, with pictures beside it. I want you to do the wrong thing with it on purpose. Do not follow it. Stand inside it instead — hear what it sounds like, feel the air on your face, smell the place, look around you. And let it get to you. Memory is physical and social a long time before it is verbal, which is exactly why a thing that happened to somebody stays while a list of letters slides straight off. The more of your senses you put in, the harder it holds. Come down to the harbour with me and I will tell it to you there.
- Its name: the hum plus the fixed vowel, then a word.
- That word means **horse**.
- Listen to the name. You will write it shortly.
- Do not follow the story. Stand inside it.

## exposition write-ma
thai: ม
heading: Now write it, and keep writing it
teaches: mo-ma
narration: en Good. Pen down in the upper left corner, on the head. Take the loop round clockwise — the ordinary direction, as you already know. Without lifting the pen, carry the stroke straight down. Second loop at the bottom. Across to the other side, then straight up. Stop. You have written a Thai letter. It will look wrong. Mine looked like a drawing of a letter rather than a letter for a long while, which is simply the way through, so fill the line and let them be bad. One habit to start here and keep for the whole course: whenever you write a symbol, on its own or inside a word, say both halves of its name while your hand is moving. Your mouth and your hand then learn it together, so neither has to wait for the other afterwards. Sit with this one a while. Write it over and over, saying its name every time.
- Start at the head, upper left. Loop **clockwise**.
- Straight down. Second loop at the bottom.
- Across, then straight up. One continuous stroke.
- It will look wrong for a while. Fill the line anyway.
- Write it again and again, saying both halves of its name.

## exposition see-nu
thai: น
heading: The mouse's letter, and the stroke that makes it
teaches: no-nu
narration: en Same head, high on the left, so your pen starts where it always starts. What happens after that is where this one goes its own way. Down from the head, clockwise, exactly as before — and then it stops behaving like the horse's letter altogether. Instead of looping a second time on the left, run along the bottom and throw the second loop right out to the far corner before you come back up. ## exposition the-vowel-house

## exposition flat-tone
heading: One rule about your own voice
narration: en A rule about your own voice, now, before you build anything with it — and this is the one people fight hardest, myself very much included. Move it and you have said a different word, exactly as you would by moving a vowel in English. Everything you are about to hear sits on a flat, level tone, roughly halfway up your speaking range. - Every syllable carries a **tone**, and the tone is part of the word.
- Everything today is **flat and level**, halfway up your range.
- Fight the English habit of rising for a question or falling to end.
- Why they are flat is next lesson's whole subject.
