# Two buckets you can hear, and one you cannot

## exposition why
heading: Class stops being a list
- Every tone you produce runs through one fact about the first letter: its class. The obvious way to learn 44 classes is to memorise 44 facts.
- You do not have to. Two of the three classes are audible — say the letter and the class falls out of the sound.
- What follows re-sorts the twelve consonants you already know. Nothing here is new to read; it is new to notice.

## exposition bucket-one
heading: Bucket one — the sounds you can hum
- m, n, ng, y, r, l and w can all be held and stretched without the air ever stopping. Any Thai letter making one of those sounds is low class. Every one, no exceptions, ten in total.
- You have five of them already: ม, น, ง, ย and ว. They berth at the harbor, where the mnemonics stage them.
- That is one of the three classes learned, permanently, from a property you can test with your own voice.

## exposition bucket-two
heading: Bucket two — the stops that release nothing
- A plain stop halts the air and lets none of it out. Palm up by your lips again: g, d and b should leave it perfectly still.
- Any Thai letter making one of those is mid class — and those letters are the whole of the mid class. Nine of them, and the list has no leftovers.
- ก, ด and บ are the three you have met. They trade at the market.

## retrieval derive-two
reveal: derive-two-answer
prompt: Without looking anything up, place ง and ด. Say the class of each, and say what you listened for to get there.

## reveal derive-two-answer
retrieval: derive-two
- ง hums and can be held, so it is low class.
- ด halts the air and releases no breath, so it is mid class.
- Neither answer needed a list. The sound decided both.

## exposition bucket-three
heading: Bucket three — the one your ears cannot settle
- The sounds left over either push breath out behind them — kh, ch, th, ph — or hiss it past a narrowing — f, s, h. Letters making those sounds are split between two classes, and nothing audible tells you which side a given letter is on.
- This is the only part of the class system that is genuine memorisation, and it is the smaller part.
- Every letter of this kind you have met so far is low class: ช, ซ, พ and ฟ. The letters on the far side of the split are still ahead of you, and they are named when they arrive.

## exposition low-side
heading: State the rule from the side you use it
- Low class letters come in two kinds: the ones with a partner across that split, and the ones with no partner at all.
- If your letter has a partner, the partner is how you reach the tones a low class letter cannot produce by itself. You will use that constantly.
- If it has no partner — any of the ones you can hum — there is a separate repair, and ห is what performs it. You meet ห in its own lesson.

## retrieval which-bucket
reveal: which-bucket-answer
prompt: ฟ hisses and ม hums. One of the two you can place from its sound alone and one you cannot. Which is which, and why?

## reveal which-bucket-answer
retrieval: which-bucket
- ม hums, so bucket one places it: low class, from the sound and nothing else.
- ฟ hisses, so it lands in the split bucket and the sound settles nothing.
- You do know where ฟ sits — low class — but you know it because this lesson said so, not because you heard it. That difference is the whole point of sorting them.

## exposition naming
heading: Why every letter's name ends the same way
- A consonant's name is built to a formula: the sound the letter makes, then the vowel aaw, then a word beginning with that sound.
- maaw máa is m plus aaw, then máa, a horse. gaaw gài is g plus aaw, then gài, a chicken. Every name you have learned was assembled that way.
- That vowel is spelled with the letter อ written twice. อ arrives with a lesson of its own, and its own name — aaw àang — is the formula applied to itself.

## retrieval name-parts
reveal: name-parts-answer
prompt: daaw dèk is the name of ด. Break it into its three parts and say what each part is doing.

## reveal name-parts-answer
retrieval: name-parts
- d — the sound the letter makes when it opens a syllable.
- aaw — the vowel every consonant name uses, which is why they all rhyme.
- dèk, a child — a word starting with that sound, so the name carries its own example.

## exposition payoff
heading: Where that leaves you
- Of the twelve consonants you have met, eight place themselves: five hum, three stop without breath.
- Four sit in the split bucket. You have been told where those four land, and told that the far side of the split exists.
- From here, most new letters sort themselves the moment you hear them. The ones that breathe are the few worth memorising, and there are not many.
