## exposition khaaw-khwaai
heading: khaaw khwaai, a buffalo at the harbor
- ค is a rounded body with its head curled counter-clockwise and tucked inside the bowl. Keep the buffalo: it is wallowing in harbor mud, and the mud is what tells you the class.
- Harbor means low class. Every tone rule you already use for ช ซ พ ฟ works on ค unchanged — there is nothing new to learn about its tones.
- The sound is kh: a k with a gust of breath behind it. Hold a palm close to your lips, say คิด (khít) — to think — and you should feel the puff land.

## exposition khaaw-not-daaw
heading: ค and ด turn their heads the other way
- ด is the same rounded bowl, but its head curls clockwise, inside the rim — the spinning top from lesson three, resting on the market floor.
- ค turns that head the other way. One stroke, one direction, and it is the only thing separating a market letter from a harbor one.
- So the head direction is not decoration here. It carries the class, and the class decides the tone.

## retrieval which-way-round
reveal: which-way-round-answer
prompt: ค and ด are the same bowl. Name the difference, and say what it costs you to get it wrong.

## reveal which-way-round-answer
retrieval: which-way-round
- The head: ด curls clockwise, ค curls the other way.
- Getting it wrong moves the letter from the market to the harbor — mid class to low class — and the tone of the whole syllable moves with it.

## exposition ue-vowels
heading: The grin vowels, ึ and ื
- Both are made in a place English has no word for. Spread your lips into a wide grin, then try to say uu without letting the grin go. That sound is ue.
- ึ is the ิ brim with a small ring balanced on top: short, one beat. นึก (núek) is to call something to mind.
- ื is the ี beret with a second upright pinned beside it: the same sound held long. ยืน (yuuen) is to stand.
- Nothing about them is audible in English, so do not look for an equivalent. Find the grin and the sound follows.

## exposition the-prop
heading: Why มือ has a letter you have not met
- ื is written above its consonant, and a vowel written above needs something beneath it once the syllable closes. With a final consonant there is something: คืน (khuuen), to return, sets ื over ค and closes on น.
- With no final consonant there is nothing, so a prop is written in: มือ (muue), a hand, and คือ (khuue), the word for is.
- That prop is a real letter with a name and a job of its own, and it gets a lesson of its own. Here it is furniture. ื needs a seat when the syllable ends bare.

## retrieval seat-or-no-seat
reveal: seat-or-no-seat-answer
prompt: Two of these need the prop and one does not: a syllable reading khuue, one reading khuuen, one reading muue. Which one stands on its own, and why?

## reveal seat-or-no-seat-answer
retrieval: seat-or-no-seat
- khuuen stands on its own: คืน already closes on น.
- khuue and muue end bare, so both are written with the prop — คือ and มือ.
- The test is never the sound. It is whether a consonant follows the vowel.

## exposition more-words
heading: Words you can read now
- คุย (khui) is to chat, and พูดคุย (phûut khui) is the same idea doubled — you have had พูด since lesson five.
- ดึง (dueng) is to pull. ยืม (yuuem) is to borrow. มืด (mûuet) is dark. พืช (phûuet) is a plant.
- คน (khon) is a person, and it is the most useful word this lesson opens up. Notice that nothing is written between ค and น: Thai leaves a short vowel out there, and that rule has a lesson of its own.
