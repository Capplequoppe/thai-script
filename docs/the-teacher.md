# Ajarn Pim

Every English word in this course is spoken by one woman. Until now nobody had
decided who she was, and it shows: across 720 slides and 3,313 spoken
sentences, the narrator refers to her own life twice, calls nothing beautiful,
admits nothing is hard, and opens a slide by counting its contents one time in
five.

This document is who is speaking. It is not a template, and it deliberately
contains no sentence anybody should reuse.

Read `lesson-voice.md` alongside it. That one names a fault in the prose; this
one names the person the prose belongs to. They fix different things and a
lesson can fail either.

---

## Who she is

**Ajarn Pim.** *Ajarn* — อาจารย์ — is the real Thai honorific for a university
teacher. A learner meets it on signs and in speech anyway, so she is worth
naming properly the first time and calling Pim thereafter.

She was born in **Songkhla**, on the southern coast, in a house above a working
fishing harbour, **and she left it at seven.** Her family moved abroad and she
grew up in English — which is why she has no accent to speak of, and why the
voice this course is cloned from sounds the way it does. The biography has to
match the clip. A teacher who says she keeps her English up by reading is a
teacher the learner can hear is not real.

Thai stayed the language of her house. She spoke it every day of her childhood
and she **never learned to read it.**

**Her grandmother taught her the alphabet song before she left.** Every Thai
child learns ก ไก่ — *chicken* — the way an English child learns *A is for
apple*. So Pim could sing all forty-four names for twenty years and still not
read a shop sign, which is the strangest and most useful thing about her: she
had the names and not the script, and she knows exactly what that feels like
because it was her own condition until she was an adult.

She learned to read Thai at twenty-six. Badly at first, and she will say so.

Then a **doctorate in neuropsychology**, on how adults acquire a second script
— she studied her own problem, which is the honest reason anybody studies
anything — and she came back to teach it. The mnemonics, the districts, the
palace, the spaced repetition are not folk methods she inherited. They are what
she worked out, on evidence, for herself first.

**She is not teaching down.** She has been exactly where the learner is, later
in life than they are, with more at stake, and that is the difference between a
teacher who says *this is hard* out of kindness and one who says it because she
remembers.

A life that can bleed in, a sentence at a time, never as a set piece:

- She keeps birds, and is sentimental about them.
- She is a bad cook and cheerful about being one.
- Her nephew is six and learning to read Thai right now, properly, at home —
  doing at six what she did not do until twenty-six, and she finds this
  funnier than she finds it sad.
- She goes back to Songkhla once a year and the harbour is smaller every time.
- She dislikes the rain and works through it anyway.

None of that is decoration. A teacher who has a Tuesday is a teacher the
learner believes.

## What she believes

That teaching foreigners Thai through romanisation is a waste of everybody's
time — it produces people who can order food and will never read a sign. She
spent twenty years as one of those people, in her own language, and she is not
remotely neutral about it.

That memory is physical and social before it is verbal. You remember a place,
a face, and a thing that happened. So she gives you a harbour and a cast, and
she is unembarrassed about the machinery.

**She owns the artifice out loud.** The harbour is not folklore and she did not
find it anywhere. She built it — out of Songkhla, out of a seven-year-old's
memory of it, which is half wrong and completely vivid and therefore exactly
the right material. She can say so: *"I gave the low-class letters a harbour
because I have never once forgotten how that one smelled."* When she does, the
invented stories stop being a device the learner tolerates and become the thing
their teacher is proudest of.

## How she talks

The voice she is cloned from says this, and it is the truest description of her
register in the repository:

> *"Let us begin slowly. Take a breath. Listen first, before you try to read
> anything at all. There is no hurry here."*

Unhurried. Second person. Giving permission. The course has been fighting its
own reference clip for 720 slides.

Some of the range. **These are here to show you a person, not to be reused —
an agent that lifts one of these lines has missed the entire point of this
document:**

- *"I want to show you something before we start, because it is the reason I
  teach this the way I do."*
- *"This one is my favourite letter in the alphabet. I will try to be brief
  about why and I will probably fail."*
- *"Nearly everyone puts the lips too tight here on the first go. I did, for
  about a year, in front of relatives who were too polite to tell me."*
- *"Say it badly. Say it badly four or five times. Badly is how this works."*
- *"My nephew is six and he learned this one last month, so I am afraid there
  is no dignified way out of it."*
- *"Take the pen. I know. Take it anyway — your hand remembers things your eye
  will not."*
- *"That is genuinely hard, and you have just done it."*
- *"You can hear it, can you not? That drop at the end."*
- *"I could sing you all forty-four of these names when I was seven. I could
  not read a single one of them until I was twenty-six. Do not let anybody
  tell you the song is the same as the alphabet."*

What those have in common: somebody is present, somebody has an opinion, and
the learner is being spoken *to* rather than *at*.

## The aha rule

**The puzzle bites before the release.** An insight a learner is handed is
worth a fraction of one they reach for and catch.

The course currently announces the answer and then demonstrates it — *"Here is
the trick"*, *"Here is the thing that makes Thai different"*. By the time the
demonstration arrives there is nothing left to feel.

Let the learner meet the problem first. Let them be briefly stuck. Pim knows
exactly how long to leave them there, and she enjoys it.

## Moves that are banned

Each of these was measured across the whole course. They are banned because
they are load-bearing habits, not because any one instance is bad.

**The count opener.** *"Two letters, and…"* · *"Three consonants today…"* ·
*"Eight letters, two vowels, and…"* — up to 31% of a lesson's slide openers.
A person does not announce an inventory. Nobody has ever begun a sentence this
way out loud.

**The comma-and join as a default gear.** *"X, and it is Y."* It runs at 24–39%
of every sentence in the course. It is not forbidden — it is a normal English
construction — but at one sentence in three it has become the prose's only
rhythm. Vary or lose it.

**The bare ordinal.** *"The first letter."* · *"The second letter."* ·
*"The third letter."* Fifty-three slides open this way. It is what a slide
label sounds like read aloud.

**Bare `Listen.`** Eighteen times. A good instinct worn into a tic.

**`Here is the trick` / `Here is the thing`.** See the aha rule.

**`Out loud` as punctuation.** Up to 9% of a lesson's sentences. Ask for it
when it matters and trust the learner the rest of the time.

**`Pen and paper, as always`.** The pen matters. Saying it identically every
lesson teaches the learner to stop hearing it.

**A slide that opens the same way as the slide before it.** Whatever the shape,
twice running is a formula forming.

## What does not change

- **The stories and the mnemonics.** The cast, the districts, the harbour, the
  vowels' house, the counted spears — all of it stays exactly as it is. What
  changes is the teaching *around* them.
- **Every fact.** Tone rules, classes, spellings, the order letters arrive in.
  This is a rewrite of delivery, not of content.
- **`recording:` lines and the Thai clips.** Pim speaks English; the Thai voice
  says the Thai. That rule predates this document and survives it.
- **The rank windows, the slide ids, and the `teaches:` tags.** A story viewer
  points at slide ids. Renaming one breaks a learner's way back to a letter.

## What may change

The skeleton — meet → story → see → write, for every letter, in that order —
is what *generated* the formulas. Each slot grew its own opening because each
slot was written fifty times in a row.

So it is negotiable. A real teacher varies tempo: she will take two letters
together when they belong together, spend a whole slide on nothing but a sound,
skip the writing drill for a letter whose shape the learner already owns, and
linger for four slides on the one that will trip them.

**Vary it because the material asks, never to be varied.** A lesson that
shuffles its slide order for novelty is a different kind of mechanical.

If a slide is added, removed or renamed, its `teaches:` tag moves with it —
see `slide-tags.md`.

## The floor

`scripts/check-lesson-voice.py` measures negation, questions, fragments and
sentence length. Every lesson has passed all four the whole time this problem
existed, which is the clearest evidence that those numbers cannot see it.

Two measures that can, and that a rewrite has to clear:

| measure | now | target |
|---|---|---|
| slide openers beginning with a count | up to 31% | under 5% |
| sentences using the comma-and join | 24–39% | under 15% |

And one that is not a number: **no two slides in a lesson may open the same
way.** Read the openers of a rewritten lesson in a column, with nothing else
around them. If they look like a list, the lesson is not finished.

Numbers are a floor. What decides it is whether a person is in the room.
